How the archive works

Two things to know before opening the map: how the archive is organised, and how to move through it.

How the archive is organised

The archive holds three connected layers. No single layer stands on its own; their connection is what makes it an archive rather than a set of images.

Three source layers
ArchaeologicalBronze Age objects — frescoes, vessels, and objects from Crete and the Aegean.
↓
PublicationPrinted images — early archaeological plates and illustrations.
↓
ReceptionTextile & fashion — Fortuny, Bakst, Kaloutsi, and Chloé S/S 1994.
From image to information
ParentSource image — the full object or publication page.
↓
CropMotif unit — the extracted form placed on the map.
↓
RecordMetadata — motif, layer, object type, author, date, source.
↓
SourceOriginal image on the museum site or Wikimedia Commons.
On the kind of relation. In documented cases, a publication image is the traceable intermediary between object and design. In others, the archive records a formal affinity between crops without assuming a direct historical borrowing.

How to explore the archive

Four steps, from the whole corpus to a single documented record.

1

Visual overview

The default view shows the whole corpus as a field of visual proximity. Images placed near one another share features detected by the model; proximity alone does not establish a historical relation.

2

Filter and compare

Reorganise the corpus by broad motif group, specific motif, or corpus layer, and read one dimension of the metadata at a time.

3

Make a temporary subcollection

The lasso isolates a neighbourhood on the projection. The selected crops open together in a “Selected images” view, with their metadata available to download.

4

Open an individual record

Each crop opens to a record with its motif, classification, layer, date, and description, and a link to the full original image where available.
